The History of Rendang: How Minangkabau's Slow-Cooked Beef Conquered the World

📅 October 8, 2026⏱ 8 min read🏷 Culture
Beef rendang, slow-cooked for hours until the coconut milk caramelizes into a dark, rich coating.

In 2011, CNN ran a global poll asking readers to name the world's most delicious food. The winner was not pizza, not sushi, not tacos — it was rendang, a slow-cooked beef dish from West Sumatra, Indonesia. It won again in 2017. For Indonesians, the result was validation; for the rest of the world, it was an introduction. But rendang's story begins centuries before any poll, in the highlands of the Minangkabau people.

Born in Minangkabau

Rendang comes from the Minangkabau ethnic group of West Sumatra, one of the world's largest matrilineal societies. The dish emerged from a practical need: preserving meat in a tropical climate without refrigeration. By simmering beef for hours in coconut milk and spices until every drop of liquid evaporates, the Minangkabau created something remarkable — a dish that stays edible for weeks at room temperature.

This was not an accident. The Minangkabau practice merantau — a tradition where young men leave their villages to seek fortune elsewhere, sometimes for years. Rendang was the perfect travel food: calorie-dense, long-lasting, and a taste of home that survived the journey. Mothers packed it for sons heading to distant cities; it arrived still good.

What "Merendang" Actually Means

The word rendang comes from merendang, which describes the slow process of cooking meat in coconut milk until the liquid reduces completely. This is the critical distinction most outsiders miss: rendang is defined by its technique, not just its ingredients.

The cooking happens in stages. First comes gulai — meat simmered in spiced coconut milk, still soupy. Continue cooking and it becomes kalio — thicker, with the sauce clinging to the meat. Keep going for hours more, and the coconut milk's oils separate and fry the meat in its own spiced fat until it turns dark brown and nearly dry. That is rendang. Many dishes labeled "rendang" outside Indonesia are actually kalio — delicious, but not the real thing. (We break down the full comparison in our rendang vs. curry guide.)

The Spice Architecture

A proper rendang bumbu (spice paste) is a small pharmacy of aromatics: shallots, garlic, ginger, galangal, turmeric, lemongrass, chilies, coriander, and candlenuts, all ground together. Then comes the coconut milk — traditionally squeezed fresh, added in stages — plus tamarind, kaffir lime leaves, and turmeric leaves. No single spice dominates; the magic is in the layering, built up over four or more hours of patient stirring.

The patience test: authentic rendang cannot be rushed. Four hours is the minimum; many Minangkabau cooks go six to eight. The reward is beef so tender it surrenders at the touch of a fork, coated in caramelized coconut solids that taste like nothing else on earth.

From Ceremony to Global Fame

In Minangkabau culture, rendang is ceremonial food. It appears at weddings, religious festivals, and when honoring guests — never as an everyday dish, because it demands too much time and too many coconuts. Serving rendang signals respect; the effort is the message.

Its global journey accelerated with the Minangkabau diaspora. Padang restaurants — named after West Sumatra's capital — spread across Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, and eventually the world, carrying rendang with them. The CNN polls of 2011 and 2017 turned a beloved regional dish into a global icon, and today "rendang" is one of the first words foreigners learn about Indonesian food.

Rendang Beyond Beef

While beef is the classic, the merendang technique applies to much more. Minangkabau kitchens make rendang from chicken, duck, liver, lung, eel, and even jackfruit (rendang nangka) and cassava leaves for vegetarian versions. Each follows the same principle: slow-cook in spiced coconut milk until dry and dark. The technique is the dish.
